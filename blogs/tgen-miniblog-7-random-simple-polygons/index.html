tgen::miniblog(7): random simple polygons

2026-10-22 · Originally published on Codeforces

This is blog 7 of a series of blogs about algorithmic challenges I came across when creating tgen.

In this blog we will tackle:

  1. Given nnn distinct points that are not all collinear, order them as the vertices of a random simple polygon, in O(nlog⁡n)\mathcal O(n\log n)O(nlogn) expected time.

Building a simple polygon by recursively splitting the points.

A polygon is simple if its boundary does not intersect itself. A random permutation of the points usually has many crossings, and repeatedly removing crossings by local fixes can be slow. Instead, we will use divide and conquer to construct a noncrossing Hamiltonian path, then use this construction to build the polygon. I first saw this idea in this Codeforces comment.

The result is not uniformly random among all simple polygons through the points. Random choices only give us varied polygon shapes.

Using paths to create a polygon

For now, assume the points are in general position, meaning no three are collinear.

We first need a smaller problem: given a set of points containing two distinguished endpoints AAA and BBB, construct a simple Hamiltonian path from AAA to BBB through every point in the set.

Suppose we can solve that problem. To build a polygon, take the lexicographically smallest point AAA and largest point BBB. Split every other point by which side of the directed line ABABAB it lies on. Build one path from AAA to BBB through the points below ABABAB, and another from BBB to AAA through the points above it.

The red path runs from AAA to BBB below the dashed line ABABAB; the blue path returns from BBB to AAA above it.

Since the edges of the two paths stay on opposite sides of the dividing line, the paths meet only at their endpoints AAA and BBB. Together they form a simple polygon.

The Divide-and-Conquer Algorithm

Given a set of points and two designated endpoints AAA and BBB, we want a noncrossing path from AAA to BBB that visits every point exactly once. In later recursive calls, the points may lie on both sides of ABABAB. If there is at most one other point, the path is immediate. Otherwise:

  1. Choose a pivot CCC uniformly among the points different from AAA and BBB.
  2. Independently choose a point DDD uniformly from the interior of segment ABABAB.
  3. The line CDCDCD separates AAA from BBB. Put every remaining point on the same side as AAA or on the same side as BBB.

A recursive subproblem: blue points lie on the AAA-side of the dashed line CDCDCD, red points on the BBB-side. The hollow point DDD lies on the dotted segment ABABAB and is not an input point.

Recursively build the path from AAA to CCC through the first set, and the path from CCC to BBB through the second set. Concatenate them at CCC to obtain the required AAA-BBB path.

The same points after recursion. The blue AAA-CCC path and red CCC-BBB path stay on opposite sides of CDCDCD and meet only at CCC.

There are only finitely many positions of DDD for which CDCDCD passes through another input point, so a continuous random choice avoids all of them with probability 111.

A generated polygon through 42 points. The red AAA-BBB path and blue BBB-AAA path form its boundary; every input point is a vertex.

Collinear points

We can also polygonize any set of at least three distinct points, as long as they are not all collinear. Consecutive edges may be collinear, but crossings and overlapping edges are still forbidden.

The construction extends to collinear inputs with some additional care; we omit those details here.

A polygonization of all 49 points of a 7×77\times77×7 lattice. Collinear vertices are allowed; each point appears exactly once on the boundary.

Complexity

We analyze the version above with points in general position and continuous choices of DDD. The input points can be placed arbitrarily; the expectation is over the algorithm’s random choices.

Lemma 1: In a call with mmm non-endpoint points, with probability at least 1/61/61/6, each child has at most 5m/65m/65m/6 non-endpoint points.

Proof: Fix DDD, excluding the finitely many positions that make it collinear with two non-endpoint points. Let HHH be the points on the side of ABABAB containing the most points (choose either side in a tie). Then k=∣H∣≥m/2k=|H|\ge m/2k=∣H∣≥m/2. Order these points by angle around DDD.

Because DDD lies on ABABAB, these directions lie in an open semicircle. If CCC is the jjj-th point in this order, CDCDCD leaves j−1j-1j−1 points of HHH on one side and k−jk-jk−j on the other.

The nine points above ABABAB form HHH, numbered in angular order around DDD; the gray points are outside HHH. Here CCC has rank 555, so CDCDCD separates four blue points from four red points. Gold rings mark ranks 4,5,64,5,64,5,6: choosing any of these pivots leaves at least three points of HHH on each side.

Consider pivots whose ranks in HHH are

⌊k/3⌋+1,…,k−⌊k/3⌋.\lfloor k/3\rfloor+1,\ldots,k-\lfloor k/3\rfloor.⌊k/3⌋+1,…,k−⌊k/3⌋.

There are at least k/3≥m/6k/3\ge m/6k/3≥m/6 such pivots, so a uniform choice among all mmm candidates selects one with probability at least 1/61/61/6. Each side of CDCDCD then contains at least ⌊k/3⌋\lfloor k/3\rfloor⌊k/3⌋ points. Since CCC becomes an endpoint of both children, either child has at most

m−1−⌊k/3⌋≤m−1−⌊m/6⌋≤5m6m-1-\lfloor k/3\rfloor \le m-1-\lfloor m/6\rfloor \le \frac{5m}{6}m−1−⌊k/3⌋≤m−1−⌊m/6⌋≤65m​

non-endpoint points. This holds for every admissible fixed DDD, hence also for an independent continuous random choice of DDD.

□\square□

Theorem 1: For every input in general position, this version runs in O(nlog⁡n)\mathcal O(n\log n)O(nlogn) expected time.

Proof: Let XPX_PXP​ count the non-base calls in which point PPP is a non-endpoint. Call a split balanced if both children have at most 5/65/65/6 of the parent’s non-endpoint points. Since PPP goes to only one side of the recursion, after at most ⌈log⁡6/5n⌉\lceil\log_{6/5}n\rceil⌈log6/5​n⌉ balanced splits it reaches a base case. By Lemma 1, even conditioned on previous choices, the expected wait for each balanced split (or for PPP‘s participation to end) is at most 666 calls. Hence

E[XP]≤6⌈log⁡6/5n⌉.\mathbb E[X_P]\le 6\lceil\log_{6/5}n\rceil.E[XP​]≤6⌈log6/5​n⌉.

Charge each non-base call’s linear work equally to its non-endpoint points. The initial split and all base calls cost O(n)\mathcal O(n)O(n), so the total running time TTT satisfies

E[T]=O(n+∑PE[XP])=O(n+∑Plog⁡n)=O(nlog⁡n).\mathbb E[T] =\mathcal O\left(n+\sum_P\mathbb E[X_P]\right) =\mathcal O\left(n+\sum_P\log n\right) =\mathcal O(n\log n).E[T]=O(n+P∑​E[XP​])=O(n+P∑​logn)=O(nlogn).

□\square□

The expected O(nlog⁡n)\mathcal O(n\log n)O(nlogn) bound also extends to collinear inputs. We omit the additional case analysis to keep the proof short. An unlucky sequence of unbalanced splits can still take Θ(n2)\Theta(n^2)Θ(n2) time.

The output distribution is not uniform, and the divide-and-conquer structure may bias which polygon shapes appear. As with any specialized generator, it is best combined with other test families when the polygon shape can affect the difficulty of the problem.
